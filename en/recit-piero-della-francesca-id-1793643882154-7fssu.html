Could this palace also be a lesson in mathematics?

Piero della Francesca was both a painter and a mathematician. In The Flagellation, perspective allows him to create a striking sense of depth on a flat surface. Follow the colours to discover how this space is constructed.

1 · The ceiling — yellow
This rectangular ceiling compartment is seen in perspective: its receding sides appear to approach one another. Its shape helps us perceive the depth of the portico.

2 · The column’s axis — green
This vertical line highlights the alignment of the statue and the column to which Christ is bound. Among the sloping lines, it gives the scene a stable structure.

3 · The paving — purple
Pale marble bands organise the floor. The purple outline marks one transverse strip of paving: its sides run towards the vanishing point. The floor becomes a grid that helps us perceive distances.

4 · The outdoor space — orange
On the right, the roof edge and selected paving lines provide further clues to depth. Their extensions lead towards the same vanishing point as the portico’s edges. Geometry thus connects Christ’s scene with the three men in the foreground.

P · The vanishing point — red
The red lines extend several edges of the ceiling and floor that recede away from us. They meet at P. The blue line passing through it is the horizon line: it marks the viewer’s eye level within the depicted space.

Notice that P does not fall on Christ, but to his right, near the flagellator’s hip. The main figure in the narrative is therefore not the point towards which the perspective lines converge.

Takeaway
Piero uses geometry to construct a shared space for the two groups. It makes depth visible and contributes to the painting’s balance and uncanny stillness.

The lines are approximate educational guides: solid lines follow visible features, while dashed lines show their extensions.
