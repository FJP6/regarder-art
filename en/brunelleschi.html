Architecture · Florence · 1377–1446

Filippo Brunelleschi

Three buildings, one idea

Antiquity provides the words; the Renaissance invents a new grammar. With Brunelleschi, columns, arches and proportions organise space into a clear, rhythmic and measurable whole.

Classical vocabularyThe bayThe InnocentiSan LorenzoSanto Spirito
In a few words

A Florentine architect and sculptor, Brunelleschi transformed architecture by rediscovering the proportions and forms of Antiquity. Designer of the famous dome of Florence Cathedral, he also played an essential role in the development of geometric perspective.

01 · Learning to look

The language of classical architecture

Brunelleschi reuses the vocabulary of classical architecture, but combines its elements in a remarkably regular system.

Column and capital

The column supports the elements above; its capital marks the transition.

Round arch

A semicircular arch that gives rhythm to the openings.

Entablature and pilaster

Horizontal and vertical elements that visually structure a building.

02 · The key to understanding

The module: a repeated measure

Imagine a bay as a section of architecture whose dimensions relate to one another. At the Ospedale degli Innocenti, the width, depth and height of the portico are conceived in proportions close to a cube: not a literal cube, but a way of thinking about space.

Remember: Brunelleschi organises space by repeating coherent units, much as a musician repeats a musical phrase.
03 · Florence

Hospital of the Innocents

Ospedale degli Innocenti · Florence

The module becomes visible in the portico

A succession of regular arcades makes the rhythm immediately apparent. Columns and arches structure the façade.

👁 What to look for

Look at the distance between two columns, then compare it with the depth of the portico. The blue glazed terracotta medallions were added after Brunelleschi's original design.

Remember: The module becomes visible in the portico.
👁 Train your eye

Start with a single arch: notice its width, the height of its column and the depth of the portico. Then move to the next arch. What stands out is not an isolated decoration, but the regularity.

04 · Florence

Basilica of San Lorenzo

The module organises the nave

The pietra serena columns, pale walls and regular arches lead the eye towards the choir.

👁 What to look for

Notice how the same column–entablature–arch sequence is repeated. The decorated ceiling and some fittings date from after Brunelleschi.

Remember: The module organises the nave.
👁 Train your eye

Look straight down the nave. Columns and arches follow one another like beats in a musical bar. Their repetition draws the eye towards the far end of the church and makes the space easy to read.

05 · Florence

Basilica of Santo Spirito

The system achieves greater unity

The repetition of bays continues through the nave and side aisles; the Latin-cross plan unifies the whole.

👁 What to look for

Look for the same proportions from one bay to the next. Semicircular chapels punctuate the perimeter of the building.

Remember: The system achieves greater unity.
👁 Train your eye

Compare the nave and the side aisles: the same language of arches and columns extends in several directions. Rhythm no longer organises just a façade or a row of bays; it brings coherence.

Additional perspective

And the Duomo's dome?

Brunelleschi did more than renew architectural proportions: he also devised a remarkable technical solution for raising the great dome of Santa Maria del Fiore. A dedicated page will explain the double shell and construction without a full-scale supporting centring.

What makes it revolutionary: an architecture conceived through measurement, geometry and overall coherence, using a rediscovered classical vocabulary.

How to recognise Brunelleschi's architecture

Four clues to help you look—and distinguish these two architects.

  • The module — A single measure is repeated to give the building a clear rhythm.
  • Grey stone and pale walls — The contrast highlights arches, columns and structural divisions.
  • Classical forms — Columns, capitals and round arches structure the space.
  • Controlled perspective — The sequence of bays naturally guides the eye.
