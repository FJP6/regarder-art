Piero della Francesca builds a world of light and geometry, where figures have a powerful presence and stories retain their mystery.

c. 1415–1420 — 1492 · Painter and mathematician

In a few words

A painter in 15th-century central Italy, Piero gives geometry a tangible presence: solid figures, clear light and restrained gestures. Space is carefully ordered, yet the stories retain their mystery. Both painter and mathematician, he gives the sacred a quiet strength, where a single detail can hold our gaze.

The Baptism of Christ

Probably c. 1437–1445 · National Gallery, London

A sacred event becomes a quiet presence in a landscape filled with light.

Look closely

An ordinary human gestureBehind Christ, a man removes his tunic to prepare for baptism. There is nothing solemn about this familiar movement. Beside the divine event, it brings the scene closer to everyday life: the rite also concerns those who come after Christ. This is one possible reading of the contrast.

The first guide is displayed. Select a step to explore another detail, or choose “Artwork only” to hide the lines.

What it tells us

John the Baptist pours water over Christ, who stands still beneath the dove. Around him, a man removes his tunic and angels hold hands. Piero combines sacred solemnity with gestures close to our own experience.

Explore further · A small movement amid the stillness

The angel crowned with roses slightly opens his mouth. The National Gallery interprets this as a response to the divine manifestation. The Eastern headwear in the background has also been linked to Byzantine visitors who came to Italy for the church councils. The gestures of harmony belong to one possible historical reading of the painting, without explaining every detail.

The Flagellation of Christ

1459–1460, according to the museum · Galleria Nazionale delle Marche, Urbino

Violence in the distance, three men close to us: space connects them, but their story eludes us.

Look closely

Close to us, yet elsewhereChrist suffers in the background. Close to us, three men stand motionless. Space brings them together, but their connection to the event eludes us. This distance makes the painting so unsettling.

The first guide is displayed. Select a step to explore another detail, or choose “Artwork only” to hide the lines.

What it tells us

Christ is scourged at the back of a building inspired by Antiquity. Three men occupy the foreground, in an open space. Everything seems precisely constructed, yet their connection to the violence remains an enigma.

Explore further · An enigma that lines cannot solve

The setting, commission and identity of the three men remain debated. Links to the Montefeltro family and religious issues surrounding Constantinople are hypotheses. The covered space on the left answers the open space on the right; the surrounded Christ echoes the young man flanked by two companions. These correspondences are visible, but do not solve the enigma. Reconstructions based on the golden ratio also remain debated.

03 / LOOK

The Resurrection

c. 1460 · Museo Civico, Sansepolcro

Above the sleeping soldiers, Christ stands upright and looks at us.

Look closely

The only one looking at usThe soldiers are asleep. Christ looks directly at us. His frontal face makes us witnesses to what the guards cannot see. The scene is silent, but his presence is hard to ignore.

The first guide is displayed. Select a step to explore another detail, or choose “Artwork only” to hide the lines.

What it tells us

The fresco is in Sansepolcro’s former government palace. Christ rises from the tomb with one foot on its edge. Below him, four soldiers sleep. The scene’s force comes from this contrast between slack bodies and commanding presence.

The Montefeltro Altarpiece

1472–1474 · Pinacoteca di Brera, Milan

Beneath majestic architecture, the sacred and worldly power meet in silence.

Look closely

One egg, two storiesHanging within the shell at the back, the ostrich egg aligns with the Virgin. Brera connects it with her miraculous motherhood and with the Montefeltro emblems. The same object can evoke both the sacred and the duke’s family. Its strange presence invites us to look more closely.

The first guide is displayed. Select a step to explore another detail, or choose “Artwork only” to hide the lines.

What it tells us

The Virgin holds the sleeping Child, surrounded by saints and angels. This gathering is called a “sacra conversazione”, or sacred conversation, although no one speaks. Federico kneels in armour; a suspended egg brings sacred history and the Montefeltro story together.

Explore further · The egg seems to shift position

The architecture places the egg within the shell at the back, while its alignment makes us perceive it above the Virgin. A review of L’Homme en perspective reports Daniel Arasse’s study of this illusion. The construction can thus serve a devotional experience, beyond simply imitating reality. Brera also links the commission to the birth of the heir and Battista Sforza’s death in 1472, without treating this as a definitive explanation.

Federico da Montefeltro and Battista Sforza

c. 1473–1475 · Uffizi Gallery, Florence

Two profiles facing one another, united by a landscape: portraiture becomes an image of power and remembrance.

Front · The portraits

Look closely

Unforgettable profilesTheir silhouettes stand out clearly: jewellery and an elaborate hairstyle for Battista, a red cap and distinctive profile for Federico. As on an ancient coin, the outline asserts identity and rank, without expansive gesture or a smile.

The first guide is displayed. Select a step to explore another detail, or choose “Artwork only” to hide the lines.

What it tells us

The duke and duchess face one another in profile, as on ancient coins. He has a darker complexion; she is very pale and adorned with jewellery. The landscape continues from one panel to the other, uniting their presence.

On the other side, prestige takes the stage. The couple appears on triumphal chariots, accompanied by personifications of the virtues. Latin inscriptions celebrate their qualities. These portraits thus have two sides: identity and praise.

Battista’s pallor is sometimes linked to her death in 1472. This is one possible reading of the image, not a certain explanation for every pictorial choice.

Explore further

The same duke, two ways of appearing

Find Federico da Montefeltro in the portraits and in the Montefeltro Altarpiece. His profile makes him recognisable, but his position in the picture changes.

A posture alone transforms the representation of a ruler. Two images of power, with two different intentions.

A painter who also writes the rules

Piero does more than use perspective: he explains it in his treatise De prospectiva pingendi. Calculating a volume, depicting a form seen at an angle: behind the figures’ stillness lies a mathematician’s work. This knowledge gives bodies weight and space depth, without dispelling the pictures’ mystery.

Keep it in your eye

Recognising a painting by Piero

Clear light, bodies with weight, a carefully ordered world. And the feeling that the figures are keeping something to themselves.

01

Light makes forms clear

Faces, fabrics and stone remain clearly readable. In the Baptism, Christ’s pale skin echoes the tree trunk; the landscape extends this gentle light.

02

Figures hold their ground

Bodies appear solid, almost sculpted. Christ in the Resurrection commands attention through his upright presence above the crouching soldiers: little movement is needed to create great strength.

03

Space is part of the story

Axes, distances and architecture organise the relationships between figures. In the Flagellation, the distant Christ and the three men near us share a precisely constructed space.

04

Emotion remains restrained

Gestures are often restrained, expressions subtle. Even the violence of the Flagellation does not turn the entire scene into turmoil. This restraint gives the pictures their distinctive stillness.

These clues work together to help us recognise Piero. Perspective or bare feet alone are not enough; nor do all his works show the same signs.
