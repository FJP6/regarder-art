REGARDER L’ART · ARTISTS

Domenico Ghirlandaio

1449–1494

Where does your eye settle first, and what do you discover as you look from one figure to the next?

In Ghirlandaio’s paintings, the story unfolds through the figures’ gestures. A glance, a hand or a figure at a doorway can lead your eye from one person to the next.

In a few words

In Florence, Domenico Ghirlandaio (1449–1494) ran a workshop with his brothers Davide and Benedetto that could paint entire chapels. The young Michelangelo trained there. In the 1480s, the banker Francesco Sassetti commissioned a cycle on the life of Saint Francis for Santa Trinita. Giovanni Tornabuoni then commissioned Ghirlandaio to decorate the choir of Santa Maria Novella. Ghirlandaio also painted on panel, as in the portrait now at the Louvre.

01 · UNE ŒUVRE À REGARDER

The Birth of the Virgin

Look closely

On the right, Saint Anne lies in bed. In front of her, a woman holds the newborn Mary. Follow the stream of water from the jug down to the basin beside the baby.

Look back to the left. A young woman in brocade leads the visitors, seen in profile. Compare her upright pose with the movement of the woman pouring the water.

Understanding

Two rhythms in one room

The movement of the jug sets the pace of the care taking place in the foreground. The young woman’s upright profile and the group behind her lend the arrival a more ceremonial pace. You can read both actions at once in the same room.

The fresco belongs to the cycle of the life of Mary commissioned by Giovanni Tornabuoni. The furnishings and clothing evoke a prosperous Florentine home. The leading visitor is often identified as Ludovica Tornabuoni, the patron’s daughter. Her place in the procession shows how a family portrait could enter an episode from the apocryphal tradition.

02 · UNE ŒUVRE À REGARDER

The Massacre of the Innocents

Look closely

In the right foreground, find the woman in red pulling a soldier by the hair. Follow her arm to his head, then notice how his upper body pitches backwards.

Now look up at the great arch. The figures above the crowd are also pressed together in painted reliefs of battle scenes.

Understanding

A melee in the manner of ancient battle reliefs

The pulling arm and the body pitching backwards make two gestures read as a single action. The movement leads you to look for other figures reacting to each other in the crowded foreground. Their interlocking bodies echo the close-packed groups in the relief. The painted arch gives you a way to read the melee below.

In Matthew’s Gospel, Herod orders the killing of the young children of Bethlehem. Ghirlandaio places those who suffer and those who carry out the order in the foreground. The language of ancient battle scenes gives the confrontation between the mothers and soldiers a scale that registers across the wall.

03 · UNE ŒUVRE À REGARDER

Saint Francis Receiving the Stigmata

Look closely

In the lower part of the fresco, to the right of centre, find Francis. He kneels in his friar’s habit with his hands raised. Follow his upward gaze towards the small winged apparition in the sky.

Come back to the figure of the saint, then let your eye move to the buildings further away and the distant valley. Notice how much of the fresco the landscape occupies around the two figures.

Understanding

The landscape gives the vision a sense of distance

Francis’s kneeling body and raised hands give your eye a starting point and a direction. The same device appears in Giotto’s versions of the episode. Here, the breadth of the landscape gives the scene a sense of scale. The small apparition remains easy to find because Francis’s whole pose leads you towards it.

The fresco belongs to the cycle of the life of Saint Francis in the Sassetti Chapel. According to Franciscan tradition, the saint received the marks of Christ’s wounds at La Verna in 1224. The gesture of a man set within a landscape you can explore with your eyes makes the vision legible.

04 · UNE ŒUVRE À REGARDER

Portrait of an Old Man and a Young Boy

Look closely

Follow the boy’s eyes to the old man’s face. The man tilts his head towards the child. Look down at the small hand resting on the red garment where their two figures meet.

Above the boy, through the window on the right, a path climbs towards the hills. Let your eye return from that distance to the two faces close together.

Understanding

A relationship you can read in a glance

Their eyes meet, and the child’s hand extends that closeness through touch. The opening onto the landscape gives you a sense of distance: the two figures share the space nearest to you. The older face keeps its distinctive features, including the contours of the nose.

The Louvre’s title identifies an old man and a young boy. Who they were and how they were related remain uncertain. The hand and the meeting gazes convey a sense of mutual attention.

How to recognise Domenico Ghirlandaio?

Small gestures that tell their own story

In the Birth of the Virgin, follow the water from the jug into the basin beside the baby. Look for a small action like this elsewhere, unfolding beside the main event.

Florentines in the scene

In the Confirmation of the Franciscan Rule, look at the Florentines coming up the stairs at the bottom of the fresco. In another sacred scene, look for a group whose clothes or profiles recall the painter’s own contemporaries.

A gesture that shows you where to look

In the Stigmatisation, Francis’s raised head and hands lead your eye to the small winged figure. In another work, look for a figure whose pose points you towards a detail further away.

Individual faces that respond to each other

In the Louvre portrait, the tilt of the older face and the child’s raised eyes make you read the two faces together. In another painting, look for a glance that links two faces with markedly different features.

Key takeaways

Ghirlandaio adapts his storytelling to each scene. In the Birth of the Virgin, the arriving visitors set a different pace from the women caring for the baby. In the Massacre, arms and bodies collide like the figures in the battle relief painted on the arch. In the Stigmatisation, Francis’s posture directs your eye towards an apparition in a wide landscape. In the Louvre portrait, a hand resting on a garment and two meeting gazes suggest a bond.

Next time you look at a Ghirlandaio, find the gesture that catches your eye. Then look for who follows it, who responds and who carries on with something else.
