Architecture · Renaissance · 1404–1472

Leon Battista Alberti

Rediscovering Antiquity to invent a new architectural language

How can a façade achieve the balance of a Roman temple? Alberti transforms ancient forms into a new architectural language—and teaches us to look at buildings differently.

In a few words

Leon Battista Alberti (1404–1472) was a major humanist, writer and architectural theorist of the Italian Renaissance. He studied proportion, beauty and the organisation of buildings. His treatise De re aedificatoria helped spread a new approach to architecture inspired by ancient Rome.

His central idea: harmony comes not from piling on decoration but from the relationships between the parts of a whole.

1

Sant’Andrea — Mantua

A façade inspired by both the temple and the triumphal arch. The great central arch draws the eye; the narrower masses on either side establish the rhythm of the composition.

The rhythm: small – large – small

Look at the great central arch, then at the two narrower sections framing it. Your gaze naturally moves towards the centre: the composition is hierarchical, balanced and monumental.

Above, the triangular pediment recalls an ancient temple; the central arch evokes a Roman triumphal arch.

Look for: the pilasters, capitals, entablature and the depth of the arch.

👁 Train your eye

Mentally hide the two side sections: the great arch almost stands alone, like a triumphal arch. Then look at the masses framing it. Their visual weight anchors the central opening..

Look up: vaults and dome

The geometric coffers of the vaults recall great Roman buildings. At the centre of the photograph, light enters the dome through the windows of its drum.

A note on chronology

The dome seen today was designed in the eighteenth century by Filippo Juvarra: it must not be attributed to Alberti. It shows how the building continued to evolve after his death.

2

Santa Maria Novella — Florence

Creating a new harmony from an older church. Alberti designed the upper façade in dialogue with the existing medieval elements.

Three things to notice

  • The triangular pediment: a direct reference to an ancient temple.
  • The large scrolls: they visually connect the tall central section to the lower wings.
  • The geometric marble patterns: their shapes and proportions unify the façade.

Alberti does not erase the past: he gives it a new coherence.

👁 Train your eye

First follow the long horizontal line separating the two levels, then look up towards the triangular pediment. The two scrolls bridge the tall central section and the lower side aisles the façade becomes a unified composition.

Why is this Renaissance architecture?

Because Antiquity is no longer just a collection of motifs to copy. It becomes a source of principles: proportion, order, symmetry and the articulation of volumes. Brunelleschi explores these principles in construction; Alberti also sets them out in writing.

The building's story — the Precious Blood of Christ

According to Christian tradition, the Roman soldier Longinus, said to have pierced Christ’s side at the Crucifixion, collected earth soaked with his blood and brought it to Mantua.

The relic, whose authenticity belongs to faith and tradition, became an important object of veneration in the Middle Ages and attracted many pilgrims.

In the fifteenth century, the Gonzaga family decided to build a larger, more prestigious basilica. Alberti therefore designed a church capable of welcoming crowds, with an immense nave and large side chapels. Construction began in 1472, the year of his death.

The relic is still kept in the crypt of Sant’Andrea today.

Remember: the monumentality of Sant’Andrea is not merely an aesthetic choice. It also responds to the need to welcome pilgrims.

How to recognise Alberti's architecture

Four clues to help you look—and distinguish these two architects.

  • Proportions — Simple ratios organise façades and relate each part to the whole.
  • Antiquity reinvented — Triumphal arches, pediments and pilasters are recomposed, not merely copied.
  • Dialogue with what already exists — At Santa Maria Novella, Alberti harmonises a façade that had already been started.
  • The façade as a composition — At Sant’Andrea, a great central arch is framed by powerful masses.
