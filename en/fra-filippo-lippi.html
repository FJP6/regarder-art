Fra Filippo Lippi

A painter-monk who gave the figures of paradise the faces of everyday life.

In a few words

Fra Filippo Lippi (c. 1406–1469) grew up in Florence and joined the Carmelite order. Both a painter and a friar, he observed the faces, gestures and feelings of those around him. His Madonnas have the tenderness of real women; his angels, the spontaneity of children. Among his pupils was Botticelli, and his son Filippino Lippi also became a painter.

01 / LOOK

Madonna and Child with Two Angels

c. 1460–1465 · Uffizi Gallery, Florence

Mary lowers her eyes towards the Child. One angel almost smiles at us: in this sacred scene, the faces and gestures feel drawn from life.

Look closely

  1. Mary’s face: with lowered eyes and clasped hands, she seems absorbed in a private thought.
  2. The smiling angel in front: he turns towards us, creating an unexpected sense of intimacy.
  3. In the background: a window opens onto a landscape, giving space to this close-up scene.

Understand

Mary is no distant, solemn figure: Lippi gives her the presence of a young woman of his own time. The angel’s smile brings a touch of everyday spontaneity into a sacred image.

This human warmth would influence his pupil Botticelli.

02 / LOOK

The Feast of Herod

1452–1464 · Prato Cathedral

One setting brings together several moments of the drama. The dance, the banquet and the presentation of John the Baptist’s head create a story for the eye to follow.

Look closely

  1. Follow Salome: she appears more than once in the same setting, at different moments of the story.
  2. Study the banquet: the guests’ gestures and the architecture guide our eyes from one episode to another.
  3. Notice the contrast: elegant clothes and dancing coexist with a violent story.

Understand

Lippi uses continuous narrative: successive moments unfold within a single image. We follow the characters to piece together what happened.

The fresco belongs to the cycle devoted to Saint John the Baptist in Prato Cathedral. The book medallion reveals the story without giving it away on the page.

03 / LOOK

The Coronation of the Virgin

c. 1439–1447 · Uffizi Gallery, Florence

A crowd of saints and angels surrounds Mary. The splendour of the ceremony does not conceal the individuality of the many faces.

Look closely

  1. At the top: Mary’s coronation anchors the composition.
  2. Around her: angels and saints form a dense crowd, yet their faces and gestures differ.
  3. At the lower left: look for a friar facing us, often considered a self-portrait of the artist.

Understand

Paradise is not filled with interchangeable figures. Lippi gives this heavenly gathering individual faces and gestures, recalling people he might have observed in daily life.

The painter may even have included himself among them, quietly connecting the earthly world to the sacred scene.

How to recognise Fra Filippo Lippi

Soft, familiar faces; Madonnas who resemble young Florentine women; angels with childlike expressions; and gestures full of tenderness. In Fra Filippo Lippi’s work, sacred figures feel profoundly human.

Lucrezia Buti: did the Virgin have a familiar face?

Filippo Lippi was a Carmelite friar, yet his life did not always follow the rules of the convent. While working in Prato, he formed a relationship with Lucrezia Buti, who was associated with a religious institution in the town. Their story, retold and embellished over time, led to two children, including Filippino Lippi, who would become a painter himself.

The Madonna’s tender, recognisably human face has inspired an enticing suggestion: could Lippi have given Mary Lucrezia’s features? There is no firm evidence. Identifying a real-life model in a sacred figure is difficult, particularly when contemporary documentation is scarce.

The story nevertheless helps us look more closely. Lippi makes the sacred feel immediate and familiar: Mary is not a distant icon, the angels behave almost like children, and one meets our gaze directly. The biography enriches our response to the painting, but it cannot settle the identity of the model.

Why does a severed head appear at the banquet?

The Feast of Herod brings to a climax a conflict recounted in Matthew (14:1–12) and Mark (6:17–29). John the Baptist condemns Herod Antipas’s marriage to Herodias, his brother’s wife, and is imprisoned. During a banquet, Herodias’s daughter — traditionally named Salome — dances before the guests. Delighted, Herod promises to grant her a request. Prompted by her mother, she asks for John’s head.

Lippi does not paint a single frozen instant. He brings successive episodes together within one architectural setting. Follow the dancer, look at the guests around the table, then find the head presented on a platter. The sequence of gestures carries the narrative forward, almost like an early visual storyboard.

The contrast is unsettling: beautiful fabrics, elegant architecture and festive ceremony surround an act of brutality. That tension gives the fresco its power. We begin by admiring the spectacle and gradually discover the violence beneath it — and the terrible cost of a reckless promise.
