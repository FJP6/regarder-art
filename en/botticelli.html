Sandro Botticelli

1445–1510

At the heart of the Florentine Renaissance, Botticelli brings grace of form and flowing lines to ancient myths and Christian stories. A glance, a gesture or a strand of hair caught in the wind can stir an emotion.

In a few words

Sandro Botticelli (1445–1510), born Alessandro Filipepi, spent most of his life working in Florence. Trained by Filippo Lippi, he painted for churches and leading Florentine families, notably the Medici. He also helped decorate the Sistine Chapel in Rome. The painter of Primavera and The Birth of Venus combined the Renaissance rediscovery of antiquity with religious painting throughout his career.

01 · UNE ŒUVRE À REGARDER

Adoration of the Magi

Look closely

Why are the Medici gathered at the feet of the Christ Child? Botticelli blends the biblical story with portraits of this powerful Florentine family. Faith and family prestige meet in a single scene.

At the centre, Mary presents Jesus. Despite the crowd, the figures draw our gaze back to the Child. The kneeling elderly king is traditionally identified as Cosimo the Elder; two others may represent his sons. On the right, a fair-haired young man looks straight at us: could this be Botticelli himself?

Understanding

The Medici traditionally portrayed as kings had already died when Botticelli painted this work: their presence honours the family. Yet the patron was Gaspare del Lama, a businessman who commissioned the painting for his chapel in Santa Maria Novella. Paying tribute to the Medici also displayed his ambitions. Some portrait identifications remain uncertain.

Remember — A religious painting can also reveal the power and society of its time.

02 · UNE ŒUVRE À REGARDER

The Birth of Venus

Look closely

A shell, a nude goddess, hair blown by the wind: a few details make this painting instantly recognisable. Venus reaches the shore. Everything around her moves; she alone seems still.

On the left, the entwined winds blow towards the goddess, scattering roses. At the centre, Venus covers herself with her hand and hair, recalling ancient statues of the “modest Venus”. On the right, a young woman approaches with a flowered cloak to welcome her.

Understanding

Despite its title, the painting shows Venus arriving on land, not being born in the sea. Inspired by antiquity, this monumental nude gives mythology a striking place in Renaissance painting.

Follow the lines: the elongated neck, the curve of the body, the hair and the folds of the cloak create an almost dancing rhythm.

Remember — With Botticelli, following a contour is already a way of feeling the painting.

03 · UNE ŒUVRE À REGARDER

Primavera (Spring)

Look closely

Desire moves among nine mythological figures in this garden. To follow their gestures, start on the right.

Zephyr seizes Chloris; flowers emerge from her mouth. Beside her, Flora scatters roses: Botticelli shows two stages of a single transformation. At the centre, Venus stands slightly back, beneath blindfolded Cupid. On the left, the Three Graces dance while Mercury raises his caduceus towards the clouds.

Understanding

Primavera does not tell a single story: it brings together several mythological episodes. On the right, desire takes the form of pursuit; on the left, the Three Graces dance in harmony. Between them, Venus brings calm.

The garden is filled with recognisable plants, yet the painting’s precise meaning remains debated. We can read its gestures without solving every mystery.

Remember — Follow the hands and glances: they tell the story.

04 · UNE ŒUVRE À REGARDER

The Calumny of Apelles

Look closely

An elegant woman drags an innocent man by the hair. Can appearances be trusted? In this splendid palace, each figure embodies an idea.

On the right, a king with donkey’s ears listens to Ignorance and Suspicion. At the centre, Calumny drags her victim, accompanied by Fraud and Deceit, who arrange her hair. Envy leads her towards the king. On the left, Repentance turns towards naked Truth, who points to the sky.

Understanding

Botticelli draws on a text by Lucian of Samosata describing a now-lost painting by the Greek artist Apelles. Through words, he brings an ancient artwork back to life.

The setting is magnificent and Calumny alluring, yet this beauty conceals an injustice. Apart from the others, Truth stands naked, without disguise.

Remember — Notice who listens, who leads and who stands alone: Botticelli gives falsehood a face.

05 · À VOUS DE JOUER

You turn !

In The Birth of Venus, you recognise me instantly. In Primavera, I am more discreet, but I still occupy the centre of the scene. Who am I?

One last challenge: find another character who appears in both paintings.

How to recognise Sandro Botticelli?

Lines that bring forms to life

Outlines shape bodies, hair and clothing. Flowing curves create a graceful sense of movement, as though everything were stirred by a gentle breeze.

Graceful figures

Elongated necks, slender fingers and delicate gestures: the figures sometimes seem to be dancing.

Melancholy beauty

Faces are gentle, with thoughtful or distant gazes. Even the brightest scenes retain a certain solemnity.

An eye for detail

Flowers, jewellery, embroidery and hair are rendered with remarkable delicacy. Every detail invites a closer look.

Key takeaways

With Botticelli, follow the lines: they make bodies dance and bring emotions to the surface.
