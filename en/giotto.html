At the dawn of the Renaissance · Painting

Giotto

c. 1267–1337 · Florentine painter and architect · Late Middle Ages

When figures begin to come alive

With Giotto, figures do more than represent a sacred story: they seem to feel emotions, occupy space and interact with one another.

He does not yet paint according to the rules of 15th-century mathematical perspective. Yet he gives bodies weight, gestures meaning and scenes remarkable intensity.

In a few words

In the early 14th century, he helps transform painting: his figures acquire physical presence, gestures express feelings and religious episodes take the form of lived scenes.

Keep in mind: Giotto does not invent geometric perspective, but gives new strength to painted narrative and the representation of human beings.

A few milestones in his life

Giotto di Bondone was probably born around 1267 near Florence and died in Florence in 1337. A celebrated painter in his lifetime, he worked in several Italian cities, including Florence, Padua and Naples.

According to a tradition later recorded by Giorgio Vasari, Giotto was discovered and trained by Cimabue. This story is famous, but the details of his training are not firmly established.

A few milestones

c. 1267 — Probable birth in Tuscany.

c. 1303–1305 — Painting of the major cycle in the Scrovegni Chapel, Padua (dating debated across sources).

1334 — Appointed master of works at Florence Cathedral; the bell tower bears his name.

1337 — Death in Florence.

Giotto belongs to the late Middle Ages, often described as the “pre-Renaissance”: he prepares changes that 15th-century painters will develop.

01 · The setting

The Scrovegni Chapel: a treasure behind a modest façade

Scrovegni Chapel, Padua · Personal photograph by the site author

In Padua, the chapel commissioned by Enrico Scrovegni houses an exceptional fresco cycle painted by Giotto in the early 14th century, generally dated around 1303–1305.

The restrained exterior contrasts with walls entirely covered in images. Episodes from the lives of Joachim and Anne, Mary and Christ unfold as a story that the eye can follow.

Look closely: the difference between the exterior architecture and the interior decoration, then the way the scenes are arranged in several tiers.

A chapel conceived as a whole

The star-studded blue vault, painted frames and frescoes create a continuous visual experience. Each scene has its own frame, yet all contribute to a single programme.

General view of the interior and starry vault · Personal photograph by the site author

A story told on the walls

Giotto organises the frescoes in tiers arranged one above another : we follow the episodes like chapters in a great painted story. The sequence runs from one wall to another, rather than simply from top to bottom.

01 · Upper tier

Joachim and Anne, then Mary

The lives of Mary’s parents and episodes from Mary’s life. Here we find The Meeting at the Golden Gate, with the kiss of Joachim and Anne.

02 · Middle tiers

The life of Christ

Christ’s birth, miracles and Passion. Among the scenes is The Kiss of Judas.

03 · Lower tier

Virtues and Vices

Figures in grisaille, painted to resemble sculptures, invite reflection on human choices.

On the entrance wall: the great Last Judgement completes the programme. Above everything, the starry blue vault surrounds the visitor.

03 · A major work

The Last Judgement: reading a monumental composition

The Last Judgement, chapel’s west wall · Personal photograph by the site author

An image organised around Christ

At the upper centre, Christ the Judge dominates the composition. To his right, on the left of the image, lies the world of the saved; to his left, on the right of the image, that of the damned and Hell.

Contrasting colours, crowded figures and movement guide the eye towards these different fates.

Look closely: the circle surrounding Christ, the procession of the saved, the angels near the cross and the monstrous figure in Hell.

In the lower part of the scene, Enrico Scrovegni also appears presenting a small model of the chapel.

A CLOSER LOOK

Two kisses, two emotions

Giotto tells of two contrasting encounters: the tenderness of Joachim and Anne, then Judas’s betrayal. Gesture becomes a language.

Giotto, Scrovegni Chapel, Padua, c. 1303–1305. Reproduction: Web Gallery of Art / Wikimedia Commons (public domain).

The Kiss of Judas: betrayal

At the centre, Judas wraps Jesus in his large yellow cloak. The two faces confront one another, still amid the agitated crowd. Torches, lances and gestures converge on this encounter.

Look for the gazes, the cloak uniting the silhouettes and the contrast between calm and agitation.

The kiss of Joachim and Anne: tenderness

In The Meeting at the Golden Gate, Anne holds Joachim’s face and their cheeks touch. Hands and gazes express palpable affection: an intimate moment portrayed with remarkable humanity.

Look for the hands on the face, the eyes close together and the contact between the two figures.

Explore further · Compare two artworks

From Cimabue to Giotto: Christ’s body gains weight

Two painted crucifixes reveal a gradual development: Cimabue already seeks an expressive depiction of suffering; Giotto emphasises physical presence and the body’s weight. This is not an instant break, but a transformation of pictorial language.

Look closely: compare the shoulders, the tilt of the head, the shape of the abdomen and the legs. In Giotto, the body seems more subject to gravity. In Cimabue, the curve and drawing already express suffering powerfully.

This comparison complements the Scrovegni Chapel scenes: in Giotto, human presence asserts itself both in great painted narratives and in a single figure.

02 · Learning to see

Recognising a painting by Giotto

01

Telling stories through gestures

Figures look at one another, draw closer, reach out or lean forward. The story becomes readable through their postures.

Look at the Baptism of Christ and the Crucifixion.

02

Giving bodies weight

Draperies envelop solid forms, and the figures seem seated, standing or gathered within a shared space.

Look at the Last Supper in the lower tier.

03

Organising a story

Scenes follow one another in tiers. Buildings, rocks and architecture give the actions a setting.

Look at the frames and the scenes arranged one above another.
