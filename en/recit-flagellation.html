WHAT IS UNFOLDING BEFORE OUR EYES?

The Flagellation — From King of the Jews to Ecce Homo

The Gospel story and the mystery of Piero's painting.

Biblical sources: Matthew 27:11–31; Mark 15:1–20; Luke 23:1–25; John 18:28–19:16. Biblical quotations: King James Version (1611).

A king before Roman power

Jesus is arrested and brought before Pontius Pilate, the Roman governor of Judaea. His accusers claim, among other things, that he presents himself as the “King of the Jews”. In a province ruled by Rome, such a claim could be seen as a political threat.

Pilate questions him about his kingship. In John's Gospel, their exchange takes a philosophical turn when the governor asks: “What is truth?” (John 18:38).

Four Gospels, four accounts

All four Gospels describe Jesus appearing before Pilate, but important differences remain.

Matthew and Mark place the scourging after the decision to hand Jesus over for crucifixion. Luke reports Pilate's proposal to punish Jesus and release him, without explicitly describing a scourging being carried out. John, by contrast, places the scourging before the final sentence, while Pilate is still trying to secure Jesus' release.

“Behold the man” — Ecce Homo

In John's account, after the scourging, the soldiers crown Jesus with thorns and dress him in purple, mocking his supposed kingship.

Pilate then presents Jesus to the crowd: “Behold the man!” (John 19:5).

These words, known in Latin as Ecce Homo, gave their name to a major subject in Christian art: Christ humiliated and exposed to the gaze of others.

Piero's enigma: who are the three men?

Piero depicts the scourging but pushes Christ into the background. In the foreground, three men appear to be conversing, seemingly indifferent to the suffering.

Their identities remain uncertain. Several hypotheses have been proposed.

A religious reading. The figures might allude to people involved in the Passion, or to different kinds of responsibility for Christ's condemnation.

A political reading. Some scholars connect the painting to the fall of Constantinople in 1453 and divisions within Christendom. Christ's suffering could thus evoke a collective tragedy.

A reading linked to Urbino. The young man in the centre has sometimes been identified as Oddantonio da Montefeltro, murdered in 1444. The painting might allude to a political drama from Piero's own time.

None of these interpretations commands a consensus.

The Gospels tell us what happened to Christ. Piero leaves us with another question: what is really unfolding before our eyes?
