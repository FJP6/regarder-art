WHAT IS UNFOLDING BEFORE OUR EYES?

The Flagellation — Jesus before Pilate

The account of Jesus’ trial before Pilate helps us understand the punishment depicted by Piero della Francesca, without solving every mystery in his painting.

A man before Roman authority

Jerusalem, in the time of Pontius Pilate. Jesus has been arrested. The religious authorities who accuse him bring him before the Roman governor. The issue is political as well as religious: could a man called “King of the Jews” threaten Roman rule?

In John’s Gospel, Pilate asks, “Art thou the King of the Jews?” (John 18:33). Jesus explains that his kingdom is not of this world. Pilate then asks, “What is truth?” (John 18:38).

Pilate declares that he finds no grounds for a charge against Jesus. Yet he does not release him.

A punishment instead of a sentence?

Pilate faces demands for Jesus’ crucifixion. The Gospels differ in how they tell what happens next.

In Luke, Pilate proposes to punish Jesus and release him, but the text does not explicitly say that a scourging takes place. In John, Pilate orders Jesus to be scourged while still seeking his release.

John · 19:1

“Then Pilate therefore took Jesus, and scourged him.”

Scourging was a punishment used in the Roman world. The Gospels do not describe the blows in detail. Their restraint contrasts with the prominence the scene later acquired in Christian art.

Mockery before death

In John’s account, soldiers dress Jesus in purple and place a crown of thorns on his head. They mock the man accused of claiming kingship: “Hail, King of the Jews!” (John 19:3).

Pilate then presents Jesus to the crowd: “Behold the man!” (John 19:5). This moment, known in art as Ecce Homo, would itself become a major subject of painting. Jesus is eventually handed over for crucifixion.

Four Gospels, four perspectives

The accounts are not identical. Matthew and Mark place the scourging after Pilate’s decision to hand Jesus over. John places it before the final decision. Luke mentions a proposed punishment but does not explicitly narrate its execution.

Matthew · 27:11–31

The hearing before Pilate, the sentence and the soldiers’ mockery.

Mark · 15:1–20

An account close to Matthew, with scourging before crucifixion.

Luke · 23:1–25

Punishment is proposed; scourging is not explicitly narrated.

John · 18:28–19:16

Longer exchanges with Pilate; scourging precedes the final decision.

And Piero della Francesca?

Piero selects a moment from this story: Jesus tied to a column while men strike him. Yet the punishment takes place in the depths of a quiet architectural setting. In the foreground, three figures appear to converse, seemingly indifferent to the scene.

The Gospels tell us what is happening to Christ. They do not identify these three men. This is where the painting’s mystery begins, explored on Piero’s artist page.

Biblical sources: Matthew 27:11–31; Mark 15:1–20; Luke 23:1–25; John 18:28–19:16. Biblical quotations: King James Version (1611).
