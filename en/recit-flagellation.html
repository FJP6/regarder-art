WHAT IS UNFOLDING BEFORE OUR EYES?

The Flagellation — Christ is not at the centre of the painting

Four Gospels recount the Passion. Piero turns a familiar episode into a visual mystery.

The Gospel accounts

After his arrest, Jesus is brought before Pontius Pilate, the Roman governor of Judaea. The interrogation raises a politically charged question: does this man, called “King of the Jews”, threaten Roman authority?

The scourging appears in the Passion narratives, but the four evangelists do not recount it in exactly the same way.

Matthew · 27:26–31

Pilate has Jesus scourged and hands him over to be crucified. The soldiers then mock his kingship, clothing him in a robe and placing a crown of thorns on his head.

Mark · 15:15–20

To satisfy the crowd, Pilate has Jesus scourged before handing him over for crucifixion. The soldiers then humiliate him.

Luke · 23:13–25

Pilate repeatedly proposes to punish Jesus and release him. Luke, however, does not explicitly narrate an actual scourging.

John · 19:1–16

Pilate has Jesus scourged before the final condemnation. Wearing a crown of thorns and a purple robe, Jesus is shown to the crowd while Pilate still seeks to release him.

These distinctions matter: the Gospels relate the Passion, but differ in sequence, emphasis and narrative detail.

What Piero chooses to show

Christ is tied to a column and scourged in the background, beneath a carefully constructed classical interior. The scene unfolds on the left, within an exacting perspective.

On the right, three men stand in the foreground, apparently detached from the violence. They seem to inhabit another space — perhaps even another time. Their clothing, composure and silence compete for our attention.

Christ is at the heart of the story, but not at the centre of our gaze. This displacement is one of the painting’s most striking decisions.

The mystery of the three men

Who are they? Contemporaries of Piero? Figures associated with the court of Urbino? Symbols of a political or religious tragedy? Scholars have proposed divergent interpretations, including connections with the Montefeltro court and the fate of Constantinople.

No identification commands universal agreement. It would be misleading to present these theories as settled fact.

What we can see is beyond dispute: two distinct scenes, meticulously ordered architecture and light, and violence rendered almost silent. Perspective connects the figures without explaining their relationship.

Biblical references: Matthew 27:26–31; Mark 15:15–20; Luke 23:13–25; John 19:1–16.
